# PetSmart — Homepage and Navigation Redesign

**Role:** Senior Web Designer
**Timeline:** 2015 to 2016
**Scope:** Sitewide navigation, homepage, product listing and detail pages, campaign design

## Details

**Model** — B2C · Enterprise retail e-commerce
**Role** — Senior Web Designer
**Team** — In-house digital design team, working alongside merchandising and engineering
**Scope** — Navigation Architecture · Homepage · Listing and Detail Pages · Campaign Design
**Timeline** — 2015 to 2016

## The Numbers

**10%** increase in navigation menu usage
**20%** increase in purchases of PetSmart's in-house brands
**1M+** pages restructured under a new navigation system
**2 years** on the site's highest-traffic pages

## The Problem

petsmart.com was over a million pages, and none of it was organized the way anyone actually shops. The navigation was built around product category first, so someone looking for dog food landed in a general pet food listing and had to scroll past cat food, bird seed, fish flakes, and reptile pellets to reach the thing they came for. Nobody thinks about their pet that way. You don't walk in with a food problem, you walk in with a dog.

So people stopped using the navigation. Most of them skipped it entirely and went straight to search, which sounds like a fine workaround until you look at what search was being asked to carry. It wasn't built to sort a million pages worth of intent, and it didn't help someone who was browsing rather than hunting for one specific product. The menu was right there. It just wasn't answering the question people were actually asking.

## Role and Constraints

I was the senior web designer on the redesign, owning the homepage, the site's single highest-trafficked page, and leading the restructure of the sitewide navigation, plus the design decisions on product listing and detail pages underneath it.

The constraint was scale. At a million pages, changing the navigation isn't a navigation project, it's a resorting of the entire catalog, and every decision ripples out across thousands of pages and a lot of people who care deeply about where their category lands. That meant nothing could be argued on instinct. If dog was going to sit at the top of the menu, the reason had to be traffic data, not my opinion about dogs.

## What I Built

I restructured the navigation around animal first, then category. Dog led, because it was the highest-traffic category on the site, with food as the first subcategory beneath it. Everything below that was ordered by real customer demand rather than by how the catalog happened to be filed internally. It's a small-sounding change that rewrote the first decision every visitor makes.

For the listing and detail pages, I ran AB tests and dug into competitive research against Walmart and Petco. The most useful thing that came out of it was a difference I didn't expect. Walmart led with price, and price drove their whole layout. PetSmart customers weren't shopping the same way. They cared more about seeing variation and options clearly, what sizes, what formulas, what's actually different between these two bags. That finding reshaped how we structured the detail page, pulling product variation forward instead of leading with a number.

I also owned the homepage itself and coordinated the digital campaign design for PetSmart's collaboration with Universal Studios on The Secret Life of Pets. That included a new-puppy-owner campaign that paired care articles and training guidance with a coupon package, so someone who just brought home a puppy got something genuinely useful alongside the offer rather than just being sold to.

## Results

The navigation redesign drove a 10% increase in menu usage, people using the navigation instead of routing around it. It also increased visibility for PetSmart's in-house brands, which saw a 20% increase in purchases after the change. Putting products where people were already looking turned out to matter more than any individual promotion of them.

## What I'd Push Further

I'd have treated search and navigation as one system instead of two. Customers had already voted for search by using it, and we improved the menu without ever fully closing the loop between the two. The better version of this project fixes both at once and lets each one cover for what the other is bad at.

I'd also push harder on remembering the pet. If someone tells you, through their first three clicks, that they have a dog, the site shouldn't make them say it again on every visit. The navigation got the structure right. Personalization was the obvious next layer and it stayed on the wish list.

The other thing I learned, the hard way: at that scale, the design work is the smaller half of the job. The bigger half is alignment. Every category has someone advocating for it, and everyone believes their section belongs higher in the menu. Getting to a navigation ordered by actual customer demand meant showing the data over and over until the ordering stopped being a matter of preference. I didn't understand going in how much of the work would be that, and it's a skill I've used on every large project since.
